Assessment, Action and Learning Tool: results

Assess, Act, Learn

Hospitals need to be prepared to provide care to the right child, at the right time, in the right way. This requires a system organised around a common goal.

The Assessment, Action and Learning Tool helps hospitals understand their own emergency care systems for children, to identify areas for improvement.

How to use it

  1. Assess resources. Assess equipment, supplies, space, and workforce at your hospital – overall and for each ward or unit caring for sick children.
  2. Assess environment. Assess whether your hospital's work environment supports staff to care for sick children effectively.
  3. Review results.
  4. Interpret results. Use the results to identify practical priorities for local action.
  5. Take action. Identify solutions and act.
  6. Reassess, learn and continue to improve. Meet monthly, reassess every 3-6 months.

Your data. Everything you enter is kept only in this browser on this device, and nothing is sent anywhere. The tool works offline once the page has loaded. Use Save data file regularly, and before clearing your browser data, so you can reload it later or compare a re-check with your first assessment.

More info

The theory behind the tool

Caring well for a severely unwell child depends on more than equipment and staff. It also depends on how people (staff, caregivers and bystanders) reason and respond to their environment and to each other. Equipment and staff matter, and Step 1 checks them. But they do not fully explain why care sometimes succeeds and sometimes breaks down.

Our research found three important factors that shaped effective emergency care: legitimacy, capability and connectedness.

Legitimacy

Legitimacy refers to reasoning and responses that reflect an understanding that the care of the severely ill child is urgent and should be prioritised, and that action is desirable and expected.

In practice, staff think: "This child needs immediate action." "I am expected and allowed to act."

Capability

Capability refers to staff reasoning that they are able to act, based on their knowledge, skills, confidence, and access to the resources needed.

In practice, staff think: "I know what to do, and can do it."

Connectedness

Connectedness refers to an understanding of emergency care as a shared responsibility.

In practice, staff think: "This is our responsibility." "Others will help me."

Across the patient journey

These features matter at every point in a child's journey through the hospital: entry to care, recognition of illness, response, and continuity, escalation and referral. A breakdown early in the journey tends to affect the care that follows.

How the tool uses the theory

  • Resources checks the material foundations: equipment, supplies, space and workforce.
  • Environment asks staff seven statements, and the results are shown for each feature and for each phase of the journey.
  • Interventions shows which features and phases each intervention is likely to strengthen, so that choices match the weaknesses found.

How the scores are calculated, and assumptions made

  • Facility availability. EECC Part 1 facility form: 34 items, 25 points. Paired adult and paediatric items score one point only if both are available. Availability % = points divided by 25.
  • Ward readiness. EECC Part 1 ward form: 35 items, 28 points (27 if oxytocin is marked not relevant). Readiness % = points divided by the total. Facility readiness is the mean of the ward scores for wards included in the average.
  • Ward rules applied. Equipment counted per ward needs a point only if "Always" available at night and the number working is at least 1 per 20 beds (rounded up). Other items need "Yes" and "Always". Nurse staffing scores if there is at least 1 nurse per 10 patients on the ward that day. The full vital-signs item needs all seven listed vital signs and "Always".
  • Unanswered items score no point until answered, and the completeness count shows how many are still to enter.
  • Environment scores. Each respondent's scores use the weighted formulas in the PECDA document (legitimacy, capability, connectedness) and the phase formulas (entry, recognition, response, continuity, escalation and referral). If a respondent skipped a question, their scores use the questions they answered.
  • Reading medians. Approximately 1 to 2 suggests a work environment that is weakly supportive and relies on individual problem solving and initiative; 3 to 4 partly supportive but not consistent across shifts and wards; close to 5 strongly supportive. Because the bands are approximate, the tool labels a median below 2.5 as weakly, 2.5 up to 4.5 as partly, and 4.5 or more as strongly supportive. It does not apply a minimum number of respondents.
  • Reading spread. An interquartile range of about 2 or more suggests substantial differences between staff and is worth discussing. About 0 to 1 suggests similar experiences. Values in between are labelled as a moderate spread.
  • Suggested focus. The intervention page suggests the feature with the lowest median, and the earliest phase whose median is within half a point of the lowest phase. These are suggestions to start a conversation, and you can choose differently.
